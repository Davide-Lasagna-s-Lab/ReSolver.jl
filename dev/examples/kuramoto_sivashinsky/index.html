Kuramoto–Sivashinsky equation

The Kuramoto–Sivashinsky equation is the standard testbed for the computation of invariant solutions of spatio-temporally chaotic PDEs. Unlike the Lorenz system, its space-time problem is stiff in both directions, along rescaled time through the frequency and along space through a fourth-order dissipation. This page searches for relative periodic orbits on the domain studied by Cvitanović, Davidchack & Siminos (2010) and finds the shortest pre-periodic orbit of the system. It then studies the convergence of the hookstep on grids of up to $10^5$ unknowns and compares five preconditioners. The model is defined in examples/kuramoto_sivashinsky/ks.jl, and examples/kuramoto_sivashinsky/example.jl converges the orbit. The scripts convergence.jl and preconditioners.jl in examples/kuramoto_sivashinsky/scripts/ produce the other figures.

Problem

The equation

\[\partial_t u = -u\,\partial_x u - \partial_x^2 u - \partial_x^4 u , \qquad x \in [0, L) , \tag{1}\]

with periodic boundary conditions and $L = 22$, is chaotic. It is equivariant under translations $u(x) \to u(x + \ell)$ and under the reflection $u(x) \to -u(-x)$. It is Galilean invariant, $u(x, t) \to u(x - Ct, t) + C$, and it conserves the mean of $u$, set to zero here. Because of the translation symmetry, its recurrent solutions are relative periodic orbits, which repeat after a period $T$ up to a shift $\ell$. In the moving frame they are periodic, with drift speed $c = -\ell/T$. The unknowns are the field over one period, the log-frequency and the drift speed, and $\partial_x$ is the derivative along the single drift direction.

Discretisation

A KSField holds the values $u(x_j, s_l)$ on the grid

\[x_j = (j - 1)\,\frac{L}{N_x}, \qquad s_l = (l - 1)\,\frac{2\pi}{N_s}, \qquad j = 1, \dots, N_x, \quad l = 1, \dots, N_s , \tag{2}\]

with $N_x$ and $N_s$ odd, so that no unpaired Nyquist mode breaks the skew-adjointness of the spectral derivatives. A KSTransformedField holds its Fourier coefficients $\hat u_{k n}$, by a real FFT along $x$ and a full FFT along $s$, with $k = 2\pi m / L$, $0 \le m \le (N_x - 1)/2$, and $|n| \le (N_s - 1)/2$. The transforms are planned once, in a KSFFT owned by the grid. The derivatives and the linear operator $k^2 - k^4$ are applied to the coefficients, and the quadratic term on the grid. The inner product is the mean over the grid points,

\[\langle u, v\rangle = \frac{1}{N_x N_s} \sum_{j, l} u(x_j, s_l)\, v(x_j, s_l) , \tag{3}\]

the discretisation of $\frac{1}{2\pi L}\int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^L u\,v\;\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}s$, so that norms and tolerances do not depend on the resolution.

Removing the mean

The nonlinear operator is the right-hand side of (1),

\[N(u) = (k^2 - k^4)\,\hat u - \tfrac12\,\partial_x(u^2) . \tag{4}\]

The linearised operator about $U$ is $L v = (k^2 - k^4)\,v - \partial_x(U v)$, and its adjoint is $L^+ w = (k^2 - k^4)\,w + U\,\partial_x w$. Both are exact for the discrete operators, because the derivatives are exactly skew-adjoint in (3).

Galilean invariance has a consequence for the space-time problem. Adding a constant $C$ to an orbit and to the drift speed gives another solution. The pair $(\delta u, \delta c) = (C, C)$ is then a null vector of the Jacobian. A constant is orthogonal to both $\partial_s u$ and $\partial_x u$, so the phase conditions do not remove it. Every term of (4) is an $x$-derivative, so $N(u)$ has zero mean for every $u$, and the equation of the mean is void. Both defects lie along the constant field.

The search removes them with the projection demean! of ks.jl, passed to System as project=demean!. It subtracts the space-time mean of a field, an orthogonal projection in (3), and the search then runs on the fields of zero mean, as on a symmetric subspace. On these fields the condition number of $\mathcal{J}$ at the orbit below is $2.4 \times 10^{4}$, against $10^{16}$ on all fields. With the log-frequency and the drift speed, the problem has

\[N = N_x N_s + 2 \tag{5}\]

real unknowns, of which the projection fixes one, the mean. The grids used below are the following.

Rounding errors of relative size $10^{-16}$ in the quadratic term are amplified by up to $k_{\max}^4$, and on the finest grid the residual stagnates around $10^{-10}$. The convergence studies below therefore stop at $\lVert r\rVert < 10^{-9}$.

Initial guess

A trajectory of (1) is integrated with the fourth-order exponential time-differencing Runge–Kutta scheme of Kassam & Trefethen (2005), with step $0.05$, from a random initial condition of zero mean, for 400 time units after a transient of 200. Near recurrences are sought among the snapshots, every $0.25$ time units, by minimising $\lVert u(t_0 + T_0) - u(t_0 + \cdot\, ; \ell)\rVert$ over the start $t_0$, the lag $T_0 \in [12, 20]$ and the shift $\ell$. Starts where the trajectory is nearly stationary are excluded. At $L = 22$ the dynamics visits equilibria and travelling waves. Without this filter the best recurrence is a nearly steady stretch, which converges to an equilibrium rather than to an orbit. The best recurrence has relative error 0.17, with $T_0 = 20.00$ and $\ell = 0.69$. The segment from $t_0$ is integrated again on the $N_s$ rescaled times and written in the frame drifting with $c = -\ell/T_0$.

Without preconditioner every Newton system stalls at the largest Krylov space, with a relative residual between $0.3$ and $1$. The steps are least-squares solutions in a Krylov space far too small, and they reduce the residual very little, by a factor of three in 50 iterations. With the jacobian preconditioner each Newton system is solved to $10^{-3}$ in 45 to 55 Arnoldi steps, at every iteration, and the search has two phases. In the first five iterations the Newton step is longer than the trust region, with $\lVert \mathcal{B}\,\delta p\rVert$ between $0.3$ and $1.5$ against a radius of $0.1$ to $0.2$. The hookstep takes the best part of it that the trust region allows, and the residual decreases slowly, from $7.8 \times 10^{-2}$ to $1.5 \times 10^{-2}$. From the sixth iteration on the full Newton step lies inside the trust region. The residual drops to $1.2 \times 10^{-3}$, $1.5 \times 10^{-6}$, $1.2 \times 10^{-9}$ and $1.2 \times 10^{-12}$, and converges linearly with a ratio close to $\tau = 10^{-3}$ (see Tolerance of the inner solve).

The converged orbit has zero mean, zero drift speed and period $T = 20.505745$. It is the shortest pre-periodic orbit of the system, $\mathrm{PPO}_{10.25}$ in the notation of Cvitanović, Davidchack & Siminos (2010), traversed twice. Half a period later the field is the reflection of its initial state, $u(x, s + \pi) = -u(x_0 - x, s)$ with $x_0 = 12.139$, to a relative error of $4 \times 10^{-5}$ limited by the resolution of the search over $x_0$, and $T/2 = 10.2529$. The formulation does not exploit the reflection symmetry, so it finds the orbit as a periodic orbit of twice the period. The drift speed, an unknown of the search, converges to zero.

Convergence of the hookstep

As for the Lorenz system, the converged orbit is interpolated spectrally to grids refined by 1, 2, 4 and 8 in both directions. Its log-frequency and drift speed are perturbed by $0.01$ and its field by 1% on the modes $|m| \le 3$, $|n| \le 3$. With the preconditioner, Newton converges in 3 iterations on every grid. The cost in Jacobian actions grows by 5% over a 61-fold increase of the number of unknowns, so the method is mesh-independent. The computing time grows by factors between 4 and 5 between successive grids. This is close to the factor of four by which the number of unknowns grows, times the logarithm of the FFTs, so the work per unknown is nearly constant. The residual drops from $1.6 \times 10^{-2}$ to $1.0 \times 10^{-4}$, $3.5 \times 10^{-7}$ and $3 \times 10^{-10}$ to $4 \times 10^{-10}$. After the first step the convergence is linear with a ratio close to $10^{-3}$, the tolerance of the inner solve, rather than quadratic. This is the behaviour of an inexact Newton method whose forcing term is constant (Dembo, Eisenstat & Steihaug 1982). Each step reduces the residual of the linear model by $\tau$, and once the error of the linearisation, quadratic in the step, falls below that, $\tau$ sets the rate.

Each inner solve takes 141 to 168 Arnoldi steps, and the curves of the four grids nearly coincide. They descend in stages, with plateaus. In the second iteration, for instance, the relative residual stays above $0.3$ for the first 75 steps. GMRES first resolves the eigenvalues of $\mathcal{J}\mathcal{B}^{-1}$ that lie far from one, on the arcs towards $\pm\mathrm{i}$ described in Preconditioners, and only then converges at the rate set by the cluster. These eigenvalues belong to the large scales, which are the same on every grid, hence the independence of the resolution.

Without preconditioner, every inner solve exhausts the Krylov space of 200 vectors. In the first Newton iteration the relative residual of the Newton system stagnates between $0.4$ and $0.95$, and in the following ones it stays close to one. The steps are almost useless. After 12 iterations and 2400 Jacobian actions the residual has dropped by a factor of three on the base grid, and hardly at all on the finest. For the Lorenz system the unpreconditioned solve succeeds as long as the Krylov space can span the whole problem. Here the problem is larger than the Krylov space already on the base grid, with 1 619 unknowns.

The number of Newton iterations depends on the tolerance and not at all on the grid. For loose tolerances it follows the theory of inexact Newton methods closely. The residual contracts by a factor equal to $\tau$ at every iteration, 0.88 for $\tau = 0.9$, 0.50 for $\tau = 0.5$ and 0.31 for $\tau = 0.3$. The hookstep takes the full inexact Newton step, whose linear model reduces the residual by exactly $\tau$, and the linearisation error is negligible at these small residuals. For $\tau = 0.95$ the contraction is too weak to reach $10^{-9}$ within 200 iterations.

The cost in Jacobian actions has a minimum at $\tau = 10^{-3}$ on every grid, 439 to 463 actions, and is flat around it. The tolerance $10^{-2}$ costs 9% more and $10^{-4}$ costs 18% more. Tightening the tolerance beyond $10^{-5}$ changes nothing. Each inner solve then fills the whole Krylov space of 200 vectors, and the cost saturates at $3 \times 200 = 600$ actions. These searches still converge, because 200 Arnoldi steps reduce the residual of the Newton system well below what Newton needs to converge in 3 iterations. Loosening the tolerance is costlier, up to 5.5 times the minimum at $\tau = 0.9$. The minimum lies at a tighter tolerance than for the Lorenz system, where it was $10^{-4}$ with the jacobian preconditioner, but the reason is the same. With Newton converging in 3 or 4 iterations, a tighter inner solve buys nothing, and a looser one multiplies the iterations.

The computing time behaves differently on the smallest grids. On the base grid the fastest searches use loose tolerances, $\tau$ between 0.3 and 0.7, at about 0.20 s against 0.26 s at $10^{-3}$, although they need up to 2.7 times more actions. With 1 619 unknowns a Jacobian action is cheap. The time is dominated by the orthogonalisation of the Arnoldi vectors, whose cost grows with the square of the dimension of the Krylov space, and by the dense linear algebra of the hookstep. Many short Krylov spaces are then cheaper than a few long ones. As the grid is refined, the Jacobian actions become dominant and the minimum of the time moves to the minimum of the actions. On the finest grid the fastest searches use $\tau = 10^{-3}$ and $10^{-2}$, at 27 and 31 s, against 107 s at $\tau = 0.9$. For large problems, where the Jacobian action is the expensive operation, the count of actions is the relevant measure, and a tolerance between $10^{-2}$ and $10^{-4}$ is a safe choice.

Preconditioners

What the Newton system contains

The block of the Jacobian that acts on the field, the derivative of the residual with respect to the field (see Search by root finding), is

\[\mathcal{J}_u\,v = \omega\,\partial_s v - c\,\partial_x v - L\{u\}\,v , \qquad L\{u\}\,v = \underbrace{-\,\partial_x^2 v - \partial_x^4 v}_{\text{linear part of KS}} \;\underbrace{-\,\partial_x (u\,v)}_{\text{advection by the orbit}} , \tag{6}\]

with $L\{u\}$ the linearisation of (4) about the current orbit $u$. A preconditioner $\mathcal{B}$ should approximate $\mathcal{J}_u$ and be cheap to invert. All the terms of (6) but one act on each space-time Fourier mode of $v$ separately. The exception is the advection $\partial_x(u\,v)$. As a product in physical space, it couples every Fourier mode of $v$ to every other, through the modes of $u$. In the Fourier basis it is a dense matrix of the size of the whole problem, and so is $\mathcal{J}_u$. Inverting it would mean solving a linear system as large and as hard as the Newton system itself, which the preconditioner is meant to make easier. The preconditioners below leave the advection out.

Why the preconditioners are diagonal

Every Fourier mode of the space-time grid, $e_{kn} = e^{\mathrm{i}(k x + n s)}$, with wavenumber $k$ and temporal index $n$, is an eigenfunction of the derivatives, with $\partial_s \to \mathrm{i}\,n$, $\partial_x \to \mathrm{i}\,k$, $\partial_x^2 \to -k^2$ and $\partial_x^4 \to k^4$. Two simplifications make $\mathcal{J}_u$ act on each mode separately. The first drops the advection, which amounts to linearising about the mean state $u = 0$ rather than about the orbit. The second freezes the frequency and the drift speed, which change during a search, at the values $\omega_0$ and $c_0$ of the initial guess $(u_0, \log\omega_0, c_0)$, the orbit from which the preconditioner is built. Then $\mathcal{B}$ does not change during the search. What is left multiplies each mode by a number, its eigenvalue, which depends on $k$ and $n$,

\[\omega_0\,\partial_s e_{kn} - c_0\,\partial_x e_{kn} + \partial_x^2 e_{kn} + \partial_x^4 e_{kn} = \lambda_{kn}\, e_{kn} , \qquad \lambda_{kn} = \mathrm{i}(\omega_0 n - c_0 k) + k^4 - k^2 , \tag{7}\]

with $\lambda_{00} = 0$ on the mean. The projection removes that mode. The real part of $\lambda_{kn}$ comes from the linear part of the equation, the imaginary part from the time derivative and the drift.

The preconditioners are of the same kind. Each multiplies the mode $e_{kn}$ by a number, $\mathcal{B}\,e_{kn} = b_{kn}\,e_{kn}$, and its inverse divides by it. They are diagonal in Fourier, since each Fourier coefficient of $\mathcal{B}^{-1}v$ depends only on the same coefficient of $v$. In ks.jl an orbit is stored as values on the space-time grid, so KSPreconditioner applies $\mathcal{B}^{-1}$ in three steps. A two-dimensional FFT gives the space-time Fourier coefficients, each coefficient is divided by $b_{kn}$, and the inverse FFT returns to the grid. The adjoint $\mathcal{B}^{-+}$ divides by the complex conjugates $\overline{b_{kn}}$.

Five preconditioners

The five preconditioners differ in which parts of $\lambda_{kn}$ they keep, and whether they keep its phase or only its size.

namemultiplier $b_{kn}$keeps of $\mathcal{J}_u$
none$1$nothing
frequency$1 + \lvert \omega_0 n - c_0 k\rvert$the size of the time derivative in the moving frame, $\omega\,\partial_s - c\,\partial_x$
viscous$1 + k^4$the size of the fourth-order dissipation, $\partial_x^4$
linear$1 + \lvert \lambda_{kn}\rvert$the size of the whole linear part, $\lvert \lambda_{kn}\rvert$
jacobian$\lambda_{kn}$the whole linear part, phase included

The first four are real and positive, hence self-adjoint, $\mathcal{B}^{-+} = \mathcal{B}^{-1}$. The added $1$ keeps them bounded below by one, so that no mode is amplified, and invertible on the mean. The jacobian preconditioner is complex. It is the linear part (7) of $\mathcal{J}_u$ itself, the analogue of the jacobian preconditioner of the Lorenz system, since the mean of the orbit is zero and the Jacobian of the right-hand side at the mean state is the linear operator. Its multipliers are smallest on the linearly unstable wavenumbers $0 < k < 1$ at $n = 0$, between $0.075$ and $0.22$ in modulus. None vanishes except $\lambda_{00}$, which the code replaces by 1, since the projection removes the mean. None of the five contains the advection by the orbit, which below causes the eigenvalues of $\mathcal{J}\mathcal{B}^{-1}$ that lie away from one. On the parameters all five divide the log-frequency by $\lVert \omega_0\,\partial_s u_0\rVert$ and the drift speed by $\lVert \partial_x u_0\rVert$, the norms of their columns of the Jacobian. In the code, KSPreconditioner(x; kind) in ks.jl computes the multipliers once, from the initial orbit x.

Two preconditioners converge, both in 3 Newton iterations on every grid, and both at a cost that does not grow with the resolution, although the number of unknowns grows by a factor of 61. The linear one costs about 450 Jacobian actions and the jacobian one 141. The other three exhaust the Krylov space of 200 vectors in every Newton iteration without reaching the tolerance of the inner solve, and the hookstep, with truncated steps, stalls.

The eigenvalues (7) explain the costs. They lie on vertical lines in the complex plane, one for each wavenumber, at $\mathrm{Re}\,\lambda_{kn} = k^4 - k^2$, and spread along the imaginary axis up to $\pm\,\omega_0 N_s/2$. The spectrum of $\mathcal{J}$ follows this structure, with the advection $-\partial_x(u\,\cdot)$ as a perturbation. Below, $\mu$ denotes an eigenvalue of $\mathcal{J}\mathcal{B}^{-1}$.

  • Without preconditioner the eigenvalues fill a region that extends along the real axis to $\mathrm{Re}\,\mu \approx 400$, the value of $k_{\max}^4$ on the base grid, and to $\pm 7$ along the imaginary axis. On the finest grid the real extent is $1.8 \times 10^{6}$. A residual polynomial of low degree cannot be small on such a region and equal to one at the origin.
  • The frequency preconditioner divides by $1 + |\omega_0 n - c_0 k|$. It compresses the imaginary extent, and the eigenvalues of the slow modes fan towards $\pm\mathrm{i}$, as for Lorenz. The dissipation is untouched, though, and the spread along the real axis, which grows as $k_{\max}^4$, remains. The condition number is even larger than without preconditioner.
  • The viscous preconditioner divides by $1 + k^4$. It maps the dissipative part of each line to a real part of order one, and the singular values become flat. The condition number drops to $1.3 \times 10^{3}$. The time derivative of the large scales is untouched, though. For small $k$ the eigenvalues remain on lines close to the imaginary axis, up to $\pm\,\omega_0 N_s/2 \approx \pm 7.4$, on both sides of the origin. This is the spectrum of the unpreconditioned Lorenz problem, which GMRES solves only with a Krylov space as large as the problem.
  • The linear preconditioner divides by $1 + |\lambda_{kn}|$. It brings the eigenvalues to modulus of order one, and the singular values are flat, with the smallest condition number of all, $2.2 \times 10^{2}$. The division by the modulus, though, leaves the phase of $\lambda_{kn}$ in place. The eigenvalues lie on arcs $\lambda_{kn}/(1 + |\lambda_{kn}|)$, from near $1$ for the dissipative small scales, where $\lambda_{kn}$ is nearly real, towards $\pm\mathrm{i}$ for the large scales of high temporal frequency, where it is imaginary. Half of them are farther than $0.14$ from one, and 10% farther than $1.29$. GMRES must damp a polynomial on an arc that nearly reaches the origin from both sides, and needs about 150 Arnoldi steps per Newton iteration.
  • The jacobian preconditioner divides by $\lambda_{kn}$ itself. It removes the phase too, and maps the linear part of $\mathcal{J}$ to the identity. The eigenvalues form a compact, X-shaped cluster centred at one, with half of them within $0.044$ of one and 90% within $0.28$. The remaining few are spread over $-1.7 < \mathrm{Re}\,\mu < 4$ and $|\mathrm{Im}\,\mu| \approx 1.2$. They come from the advection $-\partial_x(u\,\cdot)$, which the preconditioner ignores and which is not small on the linearly unstable wavenumbers, where $\lambda_{kn}$ is. GMRES deflates these outliers in a few steps and converges in about 47 Arnoldi steps per Newton iteration. This holds although the condition number, $1.6 \times 10^{3}$, is seven times larger than with the linear preconditioner.

The advection does not spoil the independence of the resolution. Its symbol grows only as $k$, against $k^4$ for the dissipation. At large $k$ the preconditioned operator is therefore a compact perturbation of the identity, and refining the grid adds eigenvalues only to the cluster.

These results agree with those for the Lorenz system. The partial preconditioners fail for complementary reasons. Each removes the stiffness in one direction of the space-time grid and leaves the other, and a Krylov method needs both removed. The condition number does not predict the cost. The viscous preconditioner fails with a condition number close to that of the jacobian one, which is the cheapest, and the linear one, with the smallest condition number, costs three times as much as the jacobian one. A preconditioner should approximate the operator, phase included, and not just its size.

Preconditioners for L-BFGS

The preconditioner that is best for the hookstep is not the best for L-BFGS. The search of the shortest pre-periodic orbit was repeated with 300 iterations of L-BFGS. With the jacobian preconditioner it reaches $\lVert r\rVert = 7.8 \times 10^{-3}$, against $1.6 \times 10^{-3}$ with the linear one. L-BFGS works in the metric $M = \mathcal{B}^+\mathcal{B}$, which multiplies each mode by $|b_{kn}|^2 = |\lambda_{kn}|^2$. This is as small as $0.075^2 \approx 5.6 \times 10^{-3}$ on the unstable wavenumbers. The gradient $M^{-1}\nabla R$ amplifies these modes by a factor of up to 180 over the others, and the line search must shorten every step to contain them. The linear preconditioner, bounded below by one, gives a better-balanced metric (see Preconditioning). The scripts of the convergence and preconditioner studies therefore converge the orbit with L-BFGS followed by the hookstep, both with the linear preconditioner, the default of KSPreconditioner. For the hookstep alone, as in example.jl, the jacobian one is the better choice.

Cost compared with direct solvers

The matrix-free hookstep never forms the Jacobian. The alternative is Newton's method with a direct solver, which forms and factorises it. This section compares the two, in theory and in practice, with the direct method of VaPOrE.jl. Lasagna (2018) used that method for tens of thousands of orbits of the Kuramoto–Sivashinsky and Lorenz systems, and Lasagna (2020) for orbits two orders of magnitude longer than the shortest. The script is examples/kuramoto_sivashinsky/scripts/direct.jl.

The direct method

The orbit is sampled at $M$ rescaled times, and the derivative in time is a centred finite difference of order $p = 2, \dots, 10$, a stencil of $p + 1$ points. The Jacobian of the spatial right-hand side at each time is a dense $N_x \times N_x$ block, since the derivatives in space are spectral. The Newton matrix, of size $n = N_x M + 2$, is then block banded and cyclic. It has one dense block on the diagonal, $p$ scaled identities off the diagonal for the stencil, wrapped around by the periodicity, and two borders. The borders are the columns of the log-frequency and of the drift speed and the two phase conditions, here on the first time level, as in VaPOrE.jl. The matrix is factorised by sparse LU (UMFPACK), once per Newton iteration.

Accuracy of finite differences in time

Finite differences need more points in time than Fourier modes for the same accuracy. For each order, the number of times $M$ is chosen so that the finite-difference derivative of the converged orbit is accurate to $10^{-6}$. Newton's method with the direct solver, started from the spectral orbit sampled at these times, then converges to a period that agrees with the spectral one to the expected accuracy.

Theoretical cost

With $N_x$ unknowns per time and a stencil of $p + 1$ points, the matrix is banded with lower and upper bandwidths $b = (p/2)\,N_x$, apart from the wrap-around and the borders, which add a low-rank correction. Banded LU with partial pivoting costs about $2 n b (2 b) = p^2 N_x^3 M$ operations and stores about $3 b n = \tfrac32\, p\, N_x^2 M$ entries. At a fixed $M$, refining the spatial grid by two multiplies the memory by four and the operations by eight. The matrix-free hookstep costs, per Newton iteration, a number of Jacobian actions independent of the resolution, about 47 here. Each action costs a few FFTs of the space-time grid, $O(N_x N_s \log(N_x N_s))$ operations. The orthogonalisation of the Krylov basis adds $O(k^2 N_x N_s)$ operations for $k$ vectors, and the basis stores $k + 1$ vectors of $N_x N_s$ entries.

The measurements follow the theory. From one grid to the next the memory of the factors grows by 3.8, 3.9 and 4.0, and the time of the factorisation by 3.7, 5.3 and 8.3. The last factor reaches the factor of 8 of the theory, at about $2 \times 10^{10}$ operations per second on the finest grid, the efficiency of the dense kernels of the supernodal factorisation. The matrix-free method, with 47 Jacobian actions per Newton iteration on every grid, is 5 times faster on the base grid and 26 times faster on the finest, with 30 to 220 times less memory.

Spectral discretisation in time

With Fourier modes in time, as in this package, the Jacobian is dense, since the spectral derivative couples every time to every other. Its LU factorisation needs $n^2$ entries and $2n^3/3$ operations. That is $2.6 \times 10^{6}$ entries and $2.8 \times 10^{9}$ operations on the base grid, and $1.0 \times 10^{10}$ entries (78 GB) and $6.5 \times 10^{14}$ operations on the grid refined eight times in both directions. A direct solver is practical only with finite differences in time, which is what VaPOrE.jl does.

Conclusions

For a one-dimensional problem at moderate resolution, the direct solver with high-order finite differences in time is fast, robust and independent of any preconditioner. On the base grid it costs about as much as the matrix-free method. Its cost grows as $N_x^3 M$ and its memory as $N_x^2 M$, against roughly $N_x N_s \log(N_x N_s)$ and $N_x N_s$ for the matrix-free method with a good preconditioner. For a PDE in two or three spatial dimensions, $N_x$ is the number of spatial degrees of freedom, $10^{4}$ to $10^{6}$. The banded factorisation, already at 1.1 GB for 257 unknowns per time, is then out of reach, as noted in Lasagna (2018). Only matrix-free methods remain, and their cost is set by the number of Jacobian actions, and so by the preconditioner.
